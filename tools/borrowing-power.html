How it works

Rates checked 15 Oct 2026 against APRA's serviceability buffer.

Banks work out how much you can borrow by checking what's left of your take-home income after living expenses and existing debts, then finding the largest loan that amount could repay. APRA requires banks to test repayments at 3 percentage points above the loan rate (the serviceability buffer), so a 6% loan is tested at 9%.

This estimate takes your income after 2026-27 tax, the Medicare levy and any HECS/HELP repayments. It counts 80% of rental or other income and treats credit cards as fully drawn at 3.8% of the limit a month, the repayment that clears the limit in three years. Both are typical lender practice, not official rules. It then subtracts your living expenses and other loan repayments.

From 1 February 2026, APRA limits each bank to 20% of new home loans at a debt-to-income ratio of 6 or more, so very high borrowing compared with income can be harder to get. Lenders also apply their own minimum living expenses, the Household Expenditure Measure, which isn't published. Most will lend less than this estimate.

Frequently asked questions

How much can I borrow on $100,000?

On a $100,000 salary with $2,500 a month in living expenses and no other debts, a 6% loan over 30 years tested at 9% gives about $491,000. Repayments at 6% would be about $2,948 a month, and the loan would be 4.9 times income.

How much does a credit card limit reduce borrowing power?

In that example, a $10,000 limit counts as $380 a month even if you never use it. That cuts the estimate to about $444,000, around $47,000 less.

What is the APRA serviceability buffer?

Banks must check you could repay at 3 percentage points above your loan rate. Without the buffer, the $100,000 example could borrow about $659,000 instead of $491,000.

How much can a couple borrow?

Two people earning $100,000 and $80,000, with $4,000 a month in living expenses, could borrow about $966,000 at 6% over 30 years. Repayments would be about $5,797 a month, at a debt-to-income ratio of 5.4.

Does rental income count?

Yes, but lenders usually count only about 80% of it. Adding $20,000 a year in rent to the $100,000 example raises the estimate to about $604,000.
